Nervous system regulation: the skill nobody taught you

By Collin Chartier, breathwork and mental performance coach · Updated October 2, 2026 · 5 min read

You were taught how to read, how to write, how to solve equations. You were never taught how to regulate your own nervous system: the system that decides whether you feel calm or panicked, focused or scattered, resilient or burnt out.

It's the most important skill you were never taught. And it changes everything.

What is your nervous system actually doing?

Your autonomic nervous system has two main branches:

The sympathetic branch is your "fight or flight" system. It speeds up your heart rate, tenses your muscles, releases adrenaline and cortisol, and narrows your focus to whatever looks like a threat. This is the survival system that kept our ancestors alive.

The parasympathetic branch is your "rest and digest" system. It slows your heart rate, relaxes your muscles, supports digestion and recovery, and widens your focus. This is the system that lets you heal, think clearly and connect with other people.

A healthy nervous system moves fluidly between the two. Sympathetic when you need energy, focus, or to respond to real danger. Parasympathetic when you need to rest, recover, think and connect.

The problem: a lot of us spend most of the day stuck on the sympathetic side. Not because a lion is chasing us, but because the inbox is full, the phone won't stop buzzing, and we slept five and a half hours last night.

The real cost of a dysregulated nervous system

When your nervous system gets stuck in stress mode, the effects show up everywhere:

  • Anxiety and panic: Your threat detection stays on high alert, reading neutral things as danger
  • Poor sleep: A body still in alert mode at night has a hard time settling into deep, restful sleep
  • Brain fog: Chronic stress makes it harder for your prefrontal cortex, the part of your brain behind focus and decisions, to do its job
  • Digestive issues: Your body doesn't prioritize digestion when it thinks it's in danger
  • Burnout: Running on stress for months wears you down. Exhaustion, cynicism, nothing left in the tank
  • Relationship strain: A dysregulated nervous system makes you reactive, defensive, and hard to reach emotionally

This isn't weakness. It's physiology. Your body is doing exactly what it was built to do. It's just stuck in the wrong mode.

What nervous system regulation actually looks like

Nervous system regulation isn't about being calm all the time. It's about being able to shift states on purpose.

A well-regulated nervous system means:

  • You can turn it up when you need energy and performance
  • You can turn it down when you need rest and recovery
  • You bounce back from stress instead of spiraling
  • You notice your body's signals and respond before you hit a breaking point

The good news: this is a skill, and it can be trained. Your nervous system adapts to the inputs you give it. And the most direct input you have is your breath.

Why breathwork is the most direct way in

Here's what makes breathwork different from journaling, exercise or most other stress tools: breathing is the one part of your autonomic nervous system you can also control on purpose.

When you breathe in, your heart rate rises a little. When you breathe out, it slows. That slowing is driven by the vagus nerve, the main nerve of your parasympathetic system. Make your exhales long and slow and you spend more time on the slowing side. Your heart rate settles, and your body gets the signal that it's safe to stand down.

You don't have to believe in it for it to work. It's how your body is wired.

Here's what's going on:

  1. The long exhale: Extended exhales lean on the vagus nerve and nudge you toward the parasympathetic side within a few breaths
  2. CO2 tolerance: Your urge to breathe is driven mostly by carbon dioxide. People who are very sensitive to CO2 tend to hit that "I can't get enough air" feeling sooner, which is a big part of panic. Slow breathing and calm breath holds can build your tolerance
  3. Heart rate variability (HRV): Breathing at around 5.5 to 6 breaths a minute produces the largest swings in heart rate for most people. HRV is one of the best windows we have into how well your nervous system handles stress
  4. Getting your thinking brain back: Slowing down and focusing on your breath gives you something to do besides spiral, and as your body settles, it gets easier to think clearly

The right technique matters

Not all breathwork does the same thing. Different techniques push your nervous system in different directions:

For calming:

  • 4-7-8 Breathing: the long hold and long exhale make it a favorite for anxiety and sleep
  • Physiological Sigh: two inhales through the nose, then a long exhale. In a 2023 Stanford study, five minutes a day of this breath (called cyclic sighing) improved mood more than mindfulness meditation did
  • Coherent Breathing: around 5.5 breaths a minute, the sweet spot for HRV

For energizing:

  • Wim Hof Method: fast, deep breathing followed by a breath hold. It drops your CO2 and spikes adrenaline. Not for anyone prone to panic, and never in water or while driving
  • Breath of Fire: rapid, rhythmic breathing through the nose for energy and focus

For balancing:

  • Box Breathing: equal counts (4-4-4-4), popularized by former Navy SEAL Mark Divine for staying composed under pressure
  • Alternate Nostril Breathing: a slow yogic practice that a lot of people find steadying

The challenge is knowing which technique to use, and when. When you're stressed, you're not in the best state to make that call. Start here: one technique, two minutes

If you've never done breathwork for nervous system regulation, start with this.

Physiological Sigh (the quickest reset I know):

  1. Inhale through your nose
  2. At the top, take a second short inhale (a "sip" of air) through your nose
  3. Exhale slowly through your mouth for as long as is comfortable
  4. Repeat 3 to 5 times

That's it. Three to five breaths. In the Stanford study (Balban, Huberman, Spiegel and colleagues, published in Cell Reports Medicine in 2023), people who practiced five minutes of cyclic sighing a day for a month reported bigger improvements in mood than people who meditated. The second sip of air helps re-inflate small air sacs in your lungs, and the long exhale is where the calming happens.

The compound effect

Like any skill, nervous system regulation gets stronger with practice. The goal isn't one perfect session. It's a few minutes a day, most days, until shifting your state is something you can do on demand.

This is the skill that changes everything. And it starts with one breath.

This is general education, not medical advice. If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or have a history of seizures or fainting, check with your doctor before trying breath holds or fast breathing. Stop if you feel dizzy or unwell.
